How to use this library

Study the strategy, practice it, then review your mistake.

1. Name the grammar job.

If you do not know what the blank needs — verb, adjective, connector, determiner, or a full sentence — you answer by feeling. Naming the job gives you control.

2. Practice one pattern at a time.

A short Part 5 or Part 6 set should focus on one skill: word forms, connectors, or sentence insertion. Mixed timing comes later.

3. Review the exact trap.

The useful feedback is not only correct or incorrect. It names the distractor and the thinking error so you do not repeat it.

Part 5 · Incomplete Sentences

Find the grammar pattern before choosing an answer.

Part 5 rewards calm structure work. The first job is not picking a word that “sounds good.” The first job is finding what the blank must do inside a workplace sentence — email, memo, notice, or report.

Step 1

Read the stem for the function of the blank: subject, verb, modifier, connector, or determiner — before you stare at A–D.

Step 2

Classify the trap: word form, tense, agreement, preposition, connector, pronoun, or modal tone.

Step 3

Eliminate by structure, not by “what sounds good.” Wrong options often break grammar even when the vocabulary feels familiar.

Word Forms

Ask what the blank must be: noun, verb, adjective, or adverb. In emails and reports, endings often decide — professional / professionally / professionalism. Do not translate the whole sentence first; check the slot.

Verb Tenses

Hunt time markers in schedules and updates: since last quarter, by Friday, while the team was traveling. Match the timeline of the workplace event, not a tense you “like.”

Prepositions

Learn office patterns as chunks: responsible for, depend on, interested in, participate in, according to. Guessing from Spanish or Korean will cost points.

Conjunctions / Transitions

Check what the blank connects. Although and because need a clause; despite and because of need a noun phrase. In memos, however and therefore signal contrast or result — pick the logic, not the longest word.

Pronouns

Track the noun in the email or notice. Singular company, product, or deadline → it; people or plural items → they. Possessives (its / their / whose) must match ownership, not “what sounds soft.”

Subject-Verb Agreement

Ignore interruptions: The manager, along with the interns, … — the real subject is still singular. Watch each / every / the number of (singular) versus a number of (often plural) in meeting notes.

Conditionals

Policies and travel plans love conditionals: If the flight is delayed…, Unless we receive payment…. Match if-clause tense to result-clause tense. Real present → will/can; unreal present → would + past form.

Passive Voice

When the focus is the document, shipment, or decision — not who did it — expect be + past participle: invoices are reviewed, the proposal was approved. With modals: must be submitted, should be confirmed.

Comparatives / Superlatives

Cost, speed, and quality lines use more … than, less / fewer, as … as, and the most / the least. Keep the form parallel and finish the comparison — incomplete than phrases are a common trap.

Gerunds / Infinitives

After a preposition, use a gerund: look forward to meeting. After many decision verbs, use an infinitive: decided to postpone, agreed to revise. Learn the workplace verbs as pairs, not as translations.

Articles / Determiners

A singular countable workplace noun usually needs a determiner: a meeting, the agenda, this invoice, each applicant. Skip the article only when the noun is plural/generic or uncountable in that context.

Modals

Company tone lives in modals. Must / have to = obligation; should / ought to = advice; can / may = ability or permission; might / could = possibility. Pick the force the notice needs, then check the base verb that follows.

Exam habit · Pace

Inside the 75-minute Reading section, aim about 20–30 seconds per Part 5 item. Fast enough to protect Parts 6–7; slow enough to name the grammar job.

Exam habit · Never leave blank

Guess after eliminating what you can. A blank answer cannot score; a structured guess sometimes can.

Exam habit · Mark and return

If two options still fight, mark the item, move on, and return with fresh eyes after you bank easier points.

Exam habit · Don’t translate first

Do not translate the whole sentence into your first language before choosing. Read for structure: blank function → trap type → eliminate. Translation is a last check, not step one.

Part 6 · Text Completion

Read the whole text — then fill each blank with discourse in mind.

Part 6 gives you 4 short workplace texts with 4 blanks each (16 questions). Genres are the ones you already know from the office: emails, memos, notices, ads, letters, and short articles. Blanks test grammar and vocabulary plus discourse connectors and sentence insertion — so one-line answers often fail even when the grammar looks fine.

Step 1

Read the full text once for meaning — who wrote it, why, and what happens next — before you open A–D on the first blank.

Step 2

Classify each blank: word form / POS, workplace vocabulary, discourse connector, or sentence insertion.

Step 3

Eliminate using context beyond the sentence: previous and next lines, pronoun reference, and paragraph logic.

Word form / POS in passage context

The slot still needs a clear part of speech — noun, verb, adjective, or adverb — but the surrounding sentences decide which form fits the message. A word that is correct in isolation can break the email’s tone or timeline. Check the blank’s neighbors, then pick the ending that matches the job.

Vocabulary in workplace context

Options may all be “real English words.” Choose the one that matches the genre and purpose: a memo about policy, a notice about a deadline, an ad about a product. Prefer the precise office meaning over a flashy synonym that does not fit the writer’s intent.

Discourse connectors (cause, contrast, addition, sequence)

Connectors link ideas across sentences: therefore / as a result (cause → effect), however / although (contrast), in addition / furthermore (addition), first / next / finally (sequence). Read the sentence before and after the blank. Wrong polarity — contrast where you need result — is a classic miss.

Sentence insertion (coherence, pronoun/reference, topic continuity)

One blank may ask you to insert a full sentence. The winner continues the topic, matches pronouns and references (this / that / these / it / they), and keeps the paragraph’s logic. A sentence that is grammatical but jumps topics or breaks reference is wrong — even if it “sounds professional.”

Read-before-blank habit

Do not answer from one line only. Skim the paragraph (or the whole short text) first, then return to the blank. Local grammar that ignores the previous sentence is how Part 6 traps high scorers who raced Part 5 style.

Time management for Part 6

In a section mock, budget about ~12 minutes for all four texts — roughly 40–45 seconds per blank as a guide, including a quick full-text read. Spend a little more on sentence-insertion items; bank easy word-form blanks quickly.

Common traps

Watch for: (1) a local grammar fit that breaks discourse with the next sentence; (2) a connector with the wrong polarity (contrast vs. result); (3) an inserted sentence that fits grammar and vocabulary but not paragraph logic or pronoun reference. If two options “feel right,” re-read the sentence before and after.

Exam habit · Never leave blank

Guess after eliminating what you can. A blank answer cannot score; a structured guess — especially after ruling out wrong polarity — sometimes can.

Exam habit · Mark and return

If an insertion blank still fights you, mark it, finish the other three blanks in that text, then return with the full paragraph fresh in mind.

Part 6 practice rule

Study the pattern here, then drill it on a full text — not one blank in isolation. Free practice builds the read-before-blank habit without a timer. Guided practice and the timed mock need a class code; they pull from the class bank and save your score on this device. Accuracy first; pace later.
