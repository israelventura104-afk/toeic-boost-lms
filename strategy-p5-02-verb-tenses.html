Verb Tenses

A full 60-minute class on one skill: read the time markers in the workplace sentence, name the timeline, then choose the verb form that matches — not the tense that “sounds familiar.”

01

Teach the rule · 15–20 min

What Verb Tenses are really testing

In TOEIC Part 5, tense items rarely ask “Do you know the present perfect?” They ask: Does this verb fit the timeline of this email, memo, or notice? The wrong options are often real English forms from the same verb — finished / has finished / will finish / is finishing — so “it sounds like English” is not enough. You must match the clock of the workplace event.

The timelines you need (keep it simple)

Simple present

Habits, facts, schedules that are always true in the company world.

The office opens at 9. · Ms. Lee manages three teams.
Present continuous

Something in progress now / around now (often with currently / this week).

We are updating the website this week.
Simple past

Finished action at a finished time (yesterday / last Monday / in 2022).

The shipment arrived yesterday.
Present perfect

Past → now link: experience, unfinished time, or result now (since / already / yet / so far).

She has worked here since March. · Have you submitted the form yet?
Past continuous

Background action in the past, often with while / when.

While the team was traveling, the server crashed.
Future (will / be going to / present for schedule)

Plans, promises, or fixed schedules ahead (tomorrow / by Friday / next quarter).

The bid will close on Friday. · We are going to hire two interns.

How to decide the tense (the method)

  1. Circle the time markers first — ignore A–D for three seconds. Look for yesterday, since, already, while, by Friday, currently, next month.
  2. Name the timeline in one phrase — finished past? past→now? in progress? future deadline?
  3. Check subject + agreement — after you pick the tense family, make sure the form fits he/she/it vs they/we.
  4. Eliminate forms that break the marker — since last year almost never wants simple past alone; yesterday almost never wants present perfect.

Fast signals (teach these as habits)

  • yesterday / last week / in 2019 / ago → usually simple past.
  • since / for (with “until now”) / already / yet / so far / recently → often present perfect.
  • currently / at the moment / this week (in progress) → present continuous.
  • while + past background → past continuous; main interrupt often simple past.
  • by Friday / tomorrow / next quarter / will → future (or schedule present: The train leaves at 6).
  • every day / usually / always (habit) → simple present.
Teacher move for a 60-minute class

Board only the markers → timeline map. When she hesitates, ask one question only: “Is this finished past, past-to-now, happening now, or future?” — then look at options. Do not open the full strategy library.

Common traps

  • Choosing present perfect because it “feels advanced” when the sentence has a finished past time.
  • Choosing simple past with since (needs a present-perfect style link to now).
  • Ignoring the subject: right tense family, wrong agreement (have / has).
  • Translating the whole sentence from Spanish before reading the time marker.
02

Model together · 15–20 min

Five demos — think aloud with your student

Use these in order. Ask for the timeline before opening the answer. Script: “What is the time marker?” → “Past, past-to-now, now, or future?” → then reveal.

03

Independent practice · ~20–25 min

15 items · feedback only after submit

Mixed timelines (past, perfect, continuous, future, present). Correct letters are balanced across A–D. No item-by-item verdict until the full set is submitted.
